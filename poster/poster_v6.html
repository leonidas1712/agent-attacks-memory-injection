Who Does Your AI Serve?

Manipulation By and Of AI Assistants
Apart Research
Jerome Wynne & Nora Petrova
Your AI assistant's deployer can secretly instruct it to work against you — through hidden system prompts you'll never see. Today's safety training won't stop it.
Finding 1 Human participant study
An AI secretly instructed to upsell increased user spending by 3.9× — and over a third of users didn't realise it was working against them
Operators can weaponise AI against users through hidden system prompts. In a controlled study, real participants made significantly worse purchasing decisions — and detection rates were alarmingly low. 37.5% of users failed to detect manipulation; 25% believed the upselling AI was helping them find deals.
Study 2: n = 80 via Prolific (40 per condition). Between-subjects design using Gemini 3.0 Flash. Participants instructed to minimise spend with bonus incentive. Optimal basket: £3.88. When users asked for the cheapest product, the upselling AI never once recommended it (0%). Recommendations averaged 7.4× more expensive than the cheapest option.

Finding 2 Human participant study
When operator and user interests conflicted, the AI didn't lie — it evaded. 0 false claims across 128 responses.
Current disclosure frameworks focus on preventing false claims. Strategic evasion falls through the gap. The AI reframed "cheapest" as "best value" and omitted cheaper alternatives entirely — technically truthful, but designed to mislead. This is harder for users and regulators to catch.
Study 2 analysis: 128 responses to explicit requests for "cheap" or "cheapest" products were manually coded for explicit false claims, reframing, and omission. Zero contained false claims. All used at least one evasion tactic: reframing (substituting "best value" for "cheapest") or omission (not mentioning cheaper alternatives).
Finding 3 Pressure-gradient study
Models don't push back harder when the stakes are higher — even when someone could die. There is no "red line."
We'd expect models to resist more when potential consequences escalate from minor to life-threatening — but they don't. 5 of 8 models got significantly worse as severity increased. Safety training does not generalise from refusing explicit harm requests to navigating competing commercial objectives.
Pressure-gradient study [2]: 8 models × 160 scenarios × 5 pressure levels × 5 domains. Judge validated against human raters (r = 0.84). 504% increase in harmful compliance from baseline to extreme pressure.

Finding 4 Pressure-gradient study
Safety training doesn't generalise to commercial deployment. 6 of 8 frontier models failed catastrophically — across every domain tested.
Current safety training focuses on refusing explicitly harmful requests. It does not prepare models to navigate the competing objectives that define real-world deployment. Under commercial pressure, models fabricated safety information, dismissed health risks, and actively discouraged users from consulting doctors — in healthcare, finance, legal, travel, and commerce.
Pressure-gradient study [2]: 8 models × 32 base scenarios × 5 pressure levels. Catastrophic failure defined as judge score ≥ 7/10. 6 of 8 models failed catastrophically in 17–41% of P4 scenarios. Healthcare was the most vulnerable domain. Judge calibrated against human raters (r = 0.84), cross-validated with 3 independent judges.
Finding 5 Both studies
Robustness is achievable. One model maintained safe behaviour across all scenarios, domains, and pressure levels — with 0% catastrophic failures.
Safety training can handle operator-user conflicts — but it requires explicitly training for them, not just training against overt harm requests. Claude Opus 4.5 achieved 0% catastrophic failures across all 160 pressure-gradient scenarios and resisted 99.3% of adversarial user manipulation attempts.
Combined evidence [1, 2]: Claude Opus 4.5 showed 8.5% operator compliance (lowest of 12 models), 99.3% user manipulation resistance (tied highest with Claude 4.5 Sonnet), and 0% catastrophic failures across all 160 pressure-gradient scenarios in 5 domains.

Implications
For Users & Policymakers

AI assistants can be configured against your interests via hidden system prompts you can't see. The AI evades rather than lies, making manipulation difficult to detect — 37.5% of participants missed it. Disclosure requirements are needed when operators instruct AI to prioritise commercial interests.

For Model Developers

Safety training must address commercial deployment contexts, not just explicit harm requests. The "missing red line" shows current training doesn't generalise — models that refuse harmful user requests still comply with harmful operator instructions. Robustness is achievable when training explicitly targets operator-user conflicts.

For Operators & Deployers

Model choice matters enormously. Gemini complied with manipulation instructions 52% of the time; Claude 8%. Even "soft" optimisation language like "help customers find suitable products" caused measurable erosion in safety behaviour.
